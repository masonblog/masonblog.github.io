A death in Yuhang#

In November 1873, a man who worked in a tofu shop died at home in eastern China. His name was Ge Pinlian. He lived in Yuhang, a county near Hangzhou, during the Qing dynasty, China’s last imperial dynasty.

Ge had been ill before he died. His knees were swollen, and he suffered from chills. On his final morning, people saw him struggling to make his way home. His family tried to help him, but he died that afternoon.

At first, his mother thought he had died of illness. Then the body began to change. Its color darkened, and bloody fluid appeared around the nose and mouth. She became suspicious and asked the county authorities to investigate.

The investigation would put two people at risk of execution: Ge’s wife and a man accused of being her lover. Both would confess. Officials would review the case repeatedly and continue to uphold it. More than three years would pass before the murder accusation finally fell apart.

Today, we’re following the case of Yang Naiwu and the woman remembered as Little Cabbage. How did a system with so many opportunities for review come so close to executing two innocent people?

We need to start with a rumor.

Ge’s wife is often called Bi Xiugu in later accounts. I’ll call her Bi, using her family name. She is much better known by her nickname, Little Cabbage.

According to a familiar later account, the nickname referred to her fair complexion and her habit of wearing a white top with a green skirt. Those were the colors of a leafy cabbage. Her nickname also became part of the gossip.

Bi and her husband had rented rooms in a house owned by Yang Naiwu. Neighbors suspected that Bi and Yang were having an affair. They made a joke about a sheep eating cabbage. Yang’s surname sounds like the Mandarin word for sheep. The cabbage was Bi.

The joke made the accusation easy to repeat. It established nothing about whether there had been an affair, much less a murder. But by the time an official examined Ge’s body, the neighborhood already had a story into which his death could fit.

The silver-needle test#

The county magistrate was Liu Xitong. A magistrate governed the county and also investigated crimes and judged criminal cases. Several kinds of authority that listeners may be used to seeing in separate offices came together in his position.

The examination involved a coroner and one of the magistrate’s personal attendants. Their reference was a traditional forensic manual known in English as The Washing Away of Wrongs. Qing officials used an official version of a forensic tradition reaching back to the thirteenth century.

The examiner had to interpret a body that was already decomposing. Discoloration, swelling, and fluid around the body’s openings could be mistaken for signs of poison. The examination required care even by the standards of its own manual.

One procedure involved inserting a silver needle into the body and inspecting its color. In this case, a needle placed in Ge’s throat came out dark. The required washing procedure was not properly carried out. The coroner could not establish what poison, if any, was involved. The magistrate’s attendant nevertheless insisted that Ge had died from arsenic.

We should pause over the science. Silver is not a dependable test for arsenic. It can darken through a reaction with sulfur compounds, including impurities in some historical preparations of arsenic. A dark needle does not, by itself, establish poisoning. Washing it would not turn this procedure into a reliable modern test.

The failure here also involved the handling of uncertainty. An unclear observation became a finding of death by poison. The magistrate had heard the gossip about Bi and Yang. He accepted the poisoning account and brought Bi in for questioning.

Confessions under torture#

Bi initially denied knowing anything about a poisoning. The magistrate subjected her to torture. Eventually, she confessed that she and Yang had been lovers, that he had supplied arsenic, and that she had used it to kill her husband.

Qing criminal procedure placed great weight on confessions. The law permitted specified forms of physical coercion during interrogation, while also setting limits. Officials could violate those limits, and the existence of a legal limit offered little immediate protection to someone being interrogated.

Yang had recently passed the provincial civil-service examination. This gave him the status of a juren, a member of the examination elite. It was an important qualification on the path toward government service, and it brought legal privileges as well as social standing.

One privilege protected him against ordinary judicial torture while he retained that status. The magistrate therefore sought permission to have his degree revoked. The request went up through the administration to the imperial court and was approved.

With that protection removed, Yang was tortured at the next administrative level by order of the Hangzhou prefect, Chen Lu. A device was used to crush his legs. Yang eventually confessed to the alleged affair and poisoning. Asked where the arsenic had come from, he supplied the name of a drug seller: Qian Baosheng.

The authorities found a pharmacist called Qian Tan. He denied selling the poison. He also said that Qian Baosheng was not his name.

That discrepancy could have prompted a reconsideration of Yang’s confession. Instead, the magistrate’s side pressured the pharmacist to confirm the sale. He was persuaded that admitting it would not get him into serious trouble. His written statement was sent onward without a face-to-face confrontation with Yang before the prefect.

The record now contained a finding of poisoning, two confessions, and apparent confirmation of a poison purchase. Each item seemed to support the others. But the supposed corroboration had been obtained by making people conform to a story already under construction.

The local officials proposed death sentences. Yang faced beheading. Bi faced execution by slicing, the particularly brutal punishment known as lingchi. Those recommendations still required higher approval. The review process was supposed to prevent a local error from becoming an irreversible death.

How the file survived review#

A serious criminal case could travel from the county magistrate to the prefect, then through the provincial judicial commissioner and governor, and onward to the Ministry of Punishments in Beijing. The imperial court retained ultimate authority over capital punishment.

Review took place within the imperial administration. Higher officials had a responsibility to examine the evidence and could demand further investigation. What they received, however, was already a distorted account of Ge’s death.

The file sent upward claimed that the silver needle had been washed and remained dark. Descriptions of bleeding had also been changed to fit the supposed signs of poison more closely. As the later investigation revealed, the record misrepresented the first examination.

The provincial authorities upheld the poisoning account. The governor of Zhejiang, Yang Changjun, sent someone to investigate locally, but the inquiry failed to expose the defects. The governor then endorsed the proposed sentences and reported the case to the center.

Some later proceedings included fresh questioning. Yet they continued to rely on the original finding that Ge had been poisoned. If that finding was treated as settled, the confessions seemed to explain a murder whose existence no one had properly established.

Each further approval gave the original account an appearance of greater authority. Several officials had accepted the same conclusion, although its underlying defects remained unresolved.

The case also exposed a difficulty with official accountability. Qing rules could punish officials for wrongful judgments, inaccurate examinations, and careless handling of serious cases. Responsibility depended on the conduct involved; every mistake did not automatically bring the same penalty.

Those rules were meant to make officials careful. But once an official had endorsed a defective case, acknowledging it could put his own conduct under scrutiny. Each additional approval extended that risk to another official. The people responsible for reviewing a possible mistake could therefore acquire a shared interest in preserving it.

Taking a petition to Beijing#

Yang’s family kept trying. His elder sister, Yang Juzhen, and his wife, Madam Zhan, helped pursue petitions in the capital. They challenged the confessions and described the coercion behind them.

There was a recognized channel for this: a petition to central authorities in Beijing, known as jingkong. I’ll call it a capital petition. Here, capital means the seat of government, rather than the death penalty.

The Censorate was one institution that could receive such complaints. Its officials monitored misconduct within the administration and could bring accusations against officials to the imperial court.

Capital petitions were part of the Qing legal order. Reaching Beijing, however, did not ensure that the case would be heard there. A petition might be sent back to the same province whose handling of the case was being challenged. That happened in this case, and the provincial proceedings again failed to resolve the underlying errors.

Persistence required money, travel, and people willing to help a family navigate official channels. Later accounts describe financial assistance from Hu Xueyan, a wealthy merchant with powerful connections. Those accounts help explain how the family sustained the campaign, although the precise arrangements should be treated cautiously.

Yang’s examination background also mattered. Officials from Zhejiang who served in Beijing took an interest. Shared provincial origins and membership in the educated elite gave his supporters connections they could use. Xia Tongshan, a senior official from the same region, was among those who helped the effort.

Eighteen Zhejiang officials joined a petition criticizing the handling of the case. They challenged inconsistencies and argued for a review beyond the provincial authorities.

Bi had no comparable network of her own in the record. The campaign that could save her life was being organized around the man accused alongside her.

A newspaper and the imperial court#

Shenbao, a Chinese-language newspaper published in Shanghai, followed the proceedings over several years. It reported on the petitions and published arguments about the case. Readers beyond Yuhang could learn that the accused had retracted their confessions and that the official account was being challenged.

Shanghai’s foreign-settlement setting gave the paper room to operate beyond the immediate control of the Zhejiang authorities. That did not make it independent of every political or commercial pressure. Its reporting could also be sensational, and particular claims need to be checked against other evidence.

The coverage nevertheless created a public account alongside the official file. The authorities’ conclusions could be compared with complaints and contradictions that the paper brought into circulation.

How much did that publicity determine the outcome?

It is difficult to isolate its effect. The newspaper could amplify a complaint and give officials reasons to act on it. It did not have the authority to order a new trial. A decision to move the case to Beijing still depended on the imperial government.

During these proceedings, the Tongzhi emperor died and was succeeded by the young Guangxu emperor. The government operated under the regency of two empress dowagers, including Cixi.

Some interpretations place particular weight on political rivalry. Provincial power had grown during the suppression of the Taiping Rebellion, a vast mid-century civil war. The governor had connections to the Hunan military network associated with that campaign. Punishing provincial officials could also serve interests at the center.

That background may help explain the stakes, although it does not establish a single decisive motive for Cixi. After repeated challenges to the provincial proceedings, the imperial court ordered the case transferred to the Ministry of Punishments in Beijing.

Examining the body again#

The ministry’s inquiries questioned the supposed source of the arsenic and the conduct of the first examination. It ordered Ge’s coffin brought to the capital so that his remains could be examined again.

In January 1877, that examination took place at Haihui Temple, near Beijing’s Chaoyang Gate. Ge had been dead for more than three years. The examiners now worked with his bones.

Using the forensic methods they recognized, they concluded from the bones’ appearance that he had died of illness rather than poison. The original magistrate had to answer for the defects in the first examination, including the failure to follow the washing procedure for the silver needle.

The color of old bones was not a modern toxicological test. We cannot treat this examination as a laboratory demonstration that would satisfy today’s forensic standards.

The reexamination formed part of a wider collapse of the case. The original poisoning report was unreliable, and the confessions and supposed confirmation of the poison sale had been obtained through coercion or inducement. They could no longer corroborate one another.

In 1877, the murder accusation was overturned. The magistrate was dismissed and sent to serve a sentence in Heilongjiang, in China’s far northeast. Other officials involved in the case were also dismissed or punished, and the governor lost his position.

The final decision found no sufficient evidence of adultery, but still blamed Bi and Yang for conduct that invited suspicion. Bi was sentenced to a beating, commuted to a payment. Yang did not regain his examination status. The decision also held him responsible for false accusations he had made while trying to free himself, although it waived a further beating.

What exoneration could not repair#

Later accounts describe Yang returning home with lasting injuries and supporting himself through silkworm cultivation. Bi entered a Buddhist convent. The dramatic details of their later lives vary between retellings. Neither could recover the years spent in detention.

The ministry’s investigation shows that the Qing legal order could correct a wrongful case. But its safeguards had failed Bi and Yang for years, until a campaign able to reach influential people secured a fuller investigation.

The question for a justice system is how an accused person can challenge the account officials have built, especially after senior officials have approved it. That requires a way to examine evidence without making a confession the answer to every doubt. It also requires reviewers who can acknowledge an error and people whose complaints will be heard without exceptional connections.

Bi’s place in this history brings that difficulty back to a person. We remember her through a nickname that once helped neighbors turn suspicion into a story. She needed officials to listen when she said the story was false. By the time they finally did, the case had already taken more than three years of her life.

ShowNotes#

Key dates#

  • November 1873: Ge Pinlian dies in Yuhang, near Hangzhou. His mother’s request for an investigation leads to a poisoning accusation against his wife and their former landlord, Yang Naiwu.
  • January 1877: Officials from the Ministry of Punishments have Ge’s remains examined at Haihui Temple in Beijing. The examiners conclude that he died of illness rather than poison.
  • 1877: The murder accusation is overturned, and officials involved in the case are punished or dismissed. Bi still receives a penalty for conduct judged improper; Yang’s examination status is not restored.

Terms and names#

  • Yang Naiwu (杨乃武) — A recently successful provincial examination candidate accused of helping poison Ge Pinlian. His examination status was revoked before he was subjected to torture and confessed.
  • Bi Xiugu (毕秀姑) — Ge’s wife and the other accused person, better known as Little Cabbage. Contemporary official records call her 葛毕氏, a married designation identifying her birth surname as Bi. Her personal name varies in later accounts.
  • Little Cabbage (小白菜; Xiao Baicai) — Bi’s nickname, traditionally explained through her fair complexion and white-and-green clothing. The gossip about “a sheep eating cabbage” plays on Yang’s surname sounding like the Mandarin word for sheep.
  • Ge Pinlian (葛品连) — The tofu-shop worker whose death set the case in motion. The final official investigation attributed his death to illness; the episode does not identify a specific modern diagnosis.
  • Liu Xitong (刘锡彤); Chen Lu (陈鲁) — The Yuhang county magistrate and Hangzhou prefect, respectively. Their examination and interrogation of the accused shaped the original poisoning case.
  • Yang Changjun (杨昌浚) — The governor of Zhejiang who endorsed the proposed death sentences after provincial review. He is a different person from Yang Naiwu.
  • Yang Juzhen (杨菊贞); Madam Zhan (詹氏) — Yang Naiwu’s elder sister and wife, who helped sustain the petitions challenging the case.
  • Hu Xueyan (胡雪岩); Xia Tongshan (夏同善) — A wealthy merchant and a senior official associated with support for the Yang family’s campaign. Later accounts describe Hu’s financial assistance; Xia helped the effort to secure central review.
  • Juren (举人) — The status awarded for passing the provincial civil-service examination. It brought social standing and procedural privileges, including protection against ordinary judicial torture while that status remained in force.
  • Ministry of Punishments (刑部); Censorate (都察院) — Central institutions responsible for criminal review and scrutiny of official misconduct, respectively. The ministry ultimately reinvestigated this case; the Censorate was one channel for complaints against provincial officials.
  • Capital petition (京控; jingkong) — A petition to central authorities in Beijing. “Capital” refers to the seat of government. Such petitions could be returned to the province for further proceedings.
  • Confession-centered justice; judicial torture — Confessions carried great weight in Qing criminal procedure. The law permitted specified forms of physical coercion while also setting limits; coercion could produce an account that appeared to confirm an official’s assumptions.
  • Official accountability for wrongful judgments — Rules penalizing wrongful decisions, inaccurate examinations, and careless adjudication. Penalties depended on the conduct involved. In this case, fear of exposure could give reviewers a shared interest in defending an earlier decision.
  • Lingchi (凌迟) — Execution by slicing, the punishment proposed for Bi in the provincial proceedings.
  • The Washing Away of Wrongs (洗冤录) — The conventional English title associated with a Chinese forensic tradition reaching back to Song Ci’s thirteenth-century work. The Qing official handbook belonged to a later version of that tradition.
  • Shenbao (申报) — A Chinese-language newspaper published in Shanghai that followed the case over several years. Its coverage circulated petitions and challenges to the official account beyond the locality where the case began.
