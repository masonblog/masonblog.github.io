The Mignonette Case: The Legal Limits of Necessity

After an 1884 shipwreck, two sailors killed a cabin boy to survive. Why did an English court reject their necessity defence?

The decision in the lifeboat#

In July 1884, an English yacht called the Mignonette was crossing the South Atlantic on its way to Australia. On 5 July it ran into a storm and began to sink. The four men aboard abandoned ship for an open lifeboat. In the rush they took only two tins of vegetables and no fresh water. They caught a turtle and collected rainwater to stay alive.

After a week without food, they were close to starving. Captain Thomas Dudley and his mate, Edwin Stephens, put a terrible question to the sailor Edmund Brooks. Could they sacrifice one man so that the other three could eat his body and live? They never asked Richard Parker, the youngest member of the crew. He was seventeen and had already become the weakest of the four after drinking seawater and going days without food. Dudley and Stephens did not name him, but he was the one they had in mind. Brooks objected, and nothing came of the discussion that day.

On 24 July, Dudley proposed drawing lots. Brooks objected again. Later that day Dudley said that if there was still no ship in sight the next morning, they would kill Parker so the others could live. This time he named Parker and dropped the idea of a lottery. Parker had taken no part in these discussions, and nobody had asked him what he thought.

The next morning there was still no ship. Parker lay in the bottom of the boat, too weak to resist. Brooks still opposed killing him. Stephens agreed to it. Dudley prayed for forgiveness and killed Parker. Over the next few days the men ate from Parker’s body. Brooks took part despite having opposed the killing. On 29 July, four days later, a passing German vessel rescued all three. They reached Falmouth, England, in September.

Once ashore, Dudley spoke openly to the customs officers and told them how Parker had been killed. He seems to have expected them to understand once they knew what the men had faced at sea. The police arrested all three on suspicion of murder. They had survived the shipwreck and now faced a murder investigation.

The dispute in Falmouth#

Many people in Falmouth supported the men once news of the case spread. It was a port town, and people there knew the risks of going to sea. Dudley and the others had been without food for days. With no rescue in sight, waiting could have meant that nobody survived. Who could expect them to judge as calmly as those of us who had never left dry land? Some locals started collecting money to help them.

Maritime tradition also helped explain that sympathy. There were cases of shipwreck survivors living on the bodies of their companions, and of sailors in desperate circumstances drawing lots to decide who would die so the others could live. Some called it the custom of the sea. Dudley’s proposal to draw lots came out of that tradition. The case raised the question of whether the law ashore could accept such a practice.

On 13 September, The Spectator published an editorial in support of prosecution. Its author asked why a crime at sea should receive more lenient treatment than one on land. If a starving person killed someone on land to stay alive, would we let the killing go untried? Why should the dangers of seafaring create an exception? The editorial argued that people could sympathise with the men and ask that their sentences be reduced or even that they be pardoned. First, though, there should be a public trial to determine how the law treated the killing.

Other readers turned their attention to Parker. On 19 September, a reader wrote to a London newspaper asking why there was talk of a fund for the survivors and nobody had proposed a memorial for the dead boy. Another reader argued that whatever the men had suffered, they should not be treated as heroes.

Before these exchanges appeared in print, officials in Falmouth had reported the case to the Board of Trade in London. The board passed it to the Home Office. Home Secretary William Harcourt decided that the prosecution should go ahead, and the Treasury Solicitor took charge of it.

From the preliminary hearing to a special verdict#

Why did a murder prosecution end up with the Treasury Solicitor? In nineteenth-century England and Wales, local police or private parties brought most criminal cases. A private prosecutor might be the victim or another person acting on their own initiative. The central government could intervene in serious or difficult cases, and the Attorney General, the Crown’s chief legal adviser, had the power to bring or handle individual prosecutions.

In 1879 Parliament created the office of Director of Public Prosecutions. The director dealt mainly with serious or difficult cases under the Attorney General’s supervision. In March 1884 Attorney General Henry James told the House of Commons that cases carrying a possible death sentence should go to the Treasury Solicitor if no suitable local body or private party was handling them. In August, Parliament made the Treasury Solicitor the Director of Public Prosecutions as well. The change was in effect by September, when the case against Dudley and the others began.

All three survivors first appeared before local magistrates. Brooks had consistently opposed the killing. With Parker dead and the four men alone when it happened, a prosecution of Brooks would have deprived the case of an important witness. The prosecution did not proceed against him. The magistrates released him, and he later testified for the prosecution about what happened in the boat.

The Mignonette was a British ship. Section 267 of the Merchant Shipping Act 1854 gave the English courts jurisdiction in these circumstances even though the killing happened on the high seas. The Falmouth magistrates held the preliminary proceedings before the case went to trial for a felony.

The trial opened in Exeter in November 1884. The prosecution presented the charge and evidence, the defence challenged it, the judge directed the trial and explained the law, and the jury heard the evidence and returned a verdict. With a general verdict, the jury finds the facts, applies the law as explained by the judge, and says whether the defendants are guilty. With a special verdict, the jury sets out its findings of fact and leaves the court to determine guilt.

The judge was John Huddleston. As he understood the law, a murder conviction meant a mandatory death sentence. He suggested a special verdict that would leave the decision on guilt to the court. The jury agreed. Its members did not have to return a verdict of guilty that would carry the death penalty.

Can necessity justify a killing?#

After the Exeter jury returned its special verdict, the High Court heard the case in London on 4 December. Five judges sat under Lord Chief Justice John Coleridge. Huddleston, who had presided in Exeter, was one of them. The special verdict had settled the main facts. The judges in London had to decide what the law made of them.

Dudley’s counsel raised the defence of necessity. In the extreme conditions of the shipwreck, he argued, the men had killed because they saw no other way to save themselves. That left the court with three questions.

First, was their situation really so desperate that there was no other way out? The defence could point to the jury’s finding that the three men probably would have starved before rescue if they had not eaten Parker’s body. There was no vessel in sight at the time and no reasonable prospect of immediate relief. Waiting might have meant that all four died. The court recognised the danger. The men could not have known when rescue would come, and the threat of death was real. In those circumstances, the court could understand what drove them to act.

Second, even if killing one person gave the others their only chance, could the law allow it? The defence relied on an example Francis Bacon had given. Two people are drowning and a plank can support only one. One pushes the other off it to save himself. Bacon thought the act could be justified. The defence argued that where only one of two people could live, the law should allow a person to put his own life first. The court found no support for Bacon’s example in the common law. The judges also discussed self-defence, which permits the use of force when necessary to resist an attack. Parker had attacked no one. Dudley killed him with Stephens’s agreement to escape the danger of starvation, so the common law rule of self-defence did not apply.

Third, if necessity could justify a killing, who could decide which person had to die? The special verdict said that even assuming it was necessary to kill someone, there was no greater necessity to kill Parker than anyone else. Dudley had proposed drawing lots, but no lottery took place. Parker was the youngest and weakest person in the boat, and nobody had asked for his opinion. The court feared that if people facing death could decide for themselves whom to kill, the weakest would be the easiest to choose. Those making the decision would stand to benefit from it. Dudley also had a duty as captain to protect his crew in a shipwreck. Killing Parker to save himself was a breach of that duty.

Justice Grove took the argument further. If it was lawful to kill Parker and no rescue came in the following days, could the three remaining men kill another? When only two were left, could the stronger kill the weaker? A note in the law report records the argument. Followed to its end, it could mean three deaths to give the fourth person a chance of survival.

The five judges rejected the defence unanimously on 9 December 1884. On the jury’s findings, Dudley had deliberately killed Parker with Stephens’s agreement. The pressure to survive did not provide a legal justification. The court found them guilty of murder and imposed the death sentence required by law at the time.

The judges acknowledged how difficult it was to think clearly and make the right choice under such suffering. They recognised that they might not have lived up to the rule themselves in the same situation. But a common law precedent binds later courts in like cases. The judges had to consider the rule they were laying down and what it would mean beyond this lifeboat. Sympathy for the defendants could not decide the verdict.

The case set a boundary for necessity. The right to life is the foundation on which all other rights depend. We cannot put lives on a scale and say that one person’s life deserves more protection than another’s. Even in mortal danger, the law does not allow a person to kill an innocent person to save themselves. That is the boundary the court drew.

Why the Crown commuted the sentences#

After the verdict, Dudley and Stephens returned to Holloway Prison in London. They petitioned together for a pardon, and family, friends, and supporters sent letters and petitions. In September The Spectator had insisted on a public trial while leaving open the possibility of a reduced sentence or pardon. The trial was now over. Home Secretary William Harcourt had to decide what advice to give Queen Victoria. Under the system of the time, the Queen could exercise the royal prerogative of mercy on his recommendation and reduce the sentences.

Harcourt had been the one to order the prosecution. Now he had to decide whether the men should hang and, if they were to be spared, what sentence should take the place of death. Attorney General Henry James had written to say the men could be released after conviction. He saw their state of mind under starvation as grounds for leniency. Harcourt’s son Lewis worried that a long prison term would only increase public sympathy for them. Harcourt worried that a full pardon would make it hard for the government to explain why a deliberate killing of an innocent person had gone entirely unpunished.

On 12 December, Harcourt recommended six months’ imprisonment without hard labour. Dudley and Stephens heard the news in prison the next day. Dudley had already telegraphed his wife that he expected to be home soon. He had to send a second telegram to tell her the term was six months. The Queen accepted Harcourt’s advice. A London correspondent recorded that people who had expected a full pardon were surprised, though in his view the public broadly accepted the six-month term.

The murder convictions stood, and both men were spared execution. Dudley and Stephens left prison in May 1885. Stephens went back to Southampton and eventually returned to sea. He died in Hull in 1914. Brooks, after giving evidence for the prosecution, lived in the Southampton area until his death in 1919. Dudley and his family moved to Sydney, the city the Mignonette had been sailing towards. He went into business supplying ships there and died during Sydney’s plague outbreak in 1900.

From the Speluncean Explorers to later cases#

Law students were still discussing the case decades later. In 1949, the American legal scholar Lon Fuller published The Case of the Speluncean Explorers in the Harvard Law Review. Drawing on the Dudley case, he set a fictional case in a cave and wrote five judicial opinions. One judge would uphold the conviction under the letter of the law and seek executive clemency. Others looked to the purpose of the law and would acquit. Another withdrew from the decision. The article became a classic and remains a reading on necessity in criminal law courses.

The Law Commission for England and Wales also considered writing necessity into statute. In 1974 a working paper proposed a general defence of necessity, which would have displaced the rule against sacrificing an innocent person established in Dudley. In its formal report three years later, the commission changed its position. The proposals did not become law, and courts still had to turn to the Dudley case when similar questions arose.

In 1987, the House of Lords cited the case again in R v Howe. The defendant said that somebody had threatened to kill him unless he killed another person. The risk in this case came from another person’s conduct, whereas the men in the lifeboat faced a shipwreck. Relying on the principle in Dudley that the law protects innocent life, the House of Lords held that duress is no defence to murder, including for someone who assists in the killing.

In 2000, the Court of Appeal of England and Wales faced a different case. A hospital wanted to separate conjoined twins. One infant’s heart and lungs could not sustain her, so she relied on the other infant’s heart for her circulation. The strain would ultimately cause both to die. If the twins were separated, the infant who could not sustain her own life would die, and the other would have a chance of survival. Could the doctors proceed knowing what it would mean for the weaker child? Their parents opposed the operation, and the hospital applied to the court for a ruling on whether it would be lawful.

All three judges approved the operation, for different reasons. One drew an analogy with using force to defend the stronger twin. Another considered each of the conditions for necessity. The third stressed that the operation aimed to separate the children so one could live and that the other’s death was a foreseeable and unavoidable consequence. The operation went ahead. One child died and the other survived.

Parker’s memorial#

Finally, let us return to Richard Parker. In the churchyard at Pear Tree Church in Southampton, Parker’s home town, there is a memorial with his name and the date he died at sea. The inscription includes a line from the Bible: “Lay not this sin to their charge.”

The rules of necessity differ from one jurisdiction to another. The Dudley case remains an important English common law precedent. In a judgment that can sound cold, five judges protected something deeply human between people in desperate circumstances.

People#

  • Richard Parker was the seventeen-year-old cabin boy. The others never consulted him when discussing the lottery or the killing.
  • Thomas Dudley and Edwin Stephens were the captain and mate. Dudley killed Parker with Stephens’s agreement. Both were convicted of murder.
  • Edmund Brooks opposed the lottery and the killing. He later ate from Parker’s body and testified at trial.
  • John Huddleston proposed the special verdict at Exeter and sat on the panel in London. John Coleridge, the Lord Chief Justice, delivered the unanimous judgment.
  • William Harcourt, the Home Secretary, backed the prosecution and later recommended commutation. Henry James, the Attorney General, gave advice on the prosecution and on leniency after conviction.

The case and the law#

  • The Queen v Dudley and Stephens (1884) 14 QBD 273. Pages 273–275 contain the special verdict, pages 276–277 the arguments, and pages 279–288 the judgment. The note on page 288 records Justice Grove’s argument and the Crown’s commutation. Page 560 adds details of the panel and the transfer of the record. Law report facsimile · Searchable judgment
  • From special verdict to judgment. The jury set out what it found to have happened, and the court decided on those facts whether the defendants were guilty of murder. The jury’s findings on the threat to the men’s survival left it to the judges to decide whether necessity was a defence. For Huddleston’s remarks to the Exeter jury, see Edward C. Lyons, p. 250 and nn. 139–142 .
  • Jurisdiction on the high seas and authority to prosecute. Section 267 of the Merchant Shipping Act 1854 allowed offences against persons or property outside British territory by masters, crew members, or apprentices to be tried under English maritime jurisdiction if they were employed on a British ship at the time or within the previous three months. Parliament created the office of Director of Public Prosecutions in 1879 and gave it to the Treasury Solicitor in 1884. Section 16 of the Supreme Court of Judicature Act 1873 supplied the basis for transferring the assize record within the High Court. 1854 Act · 1879 Act · 1884 parliamentary debate · 1884 amendment · 1873 Act
  • The death penalty and the royal prerogative of mercy. Section 1 of the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 made death the statutory punishment for murder. On the Home Secretary’s advice, Queen Victoria reduced the sentences. The convictions stood. After the judgment#

  • 1949: Lon Fuller, The Case of the Speluncean Explorers. Fuller moves the lifeboat’s dilemma into a cave and has five judges write different opinions. The story explores the relationship between legal interpretation, criminal liability, and executive clemency. Original article, 62 Harv. L. Rev. 616
  • 1974–1977: Law Commission for England and Wales. Working Paper No. 55 proposed a general defence of necessity and provisionally left murder within its scope. Report No. 83 changed course and recommended against creating a general defence. The proposals were never enacted. 1974 working paper, paras. 52 and 57 · 1977 report, paras. 4.33 and 6.4 · Implementation record
  • R v Howe [1987] AC 417. Citing Dudley, the House of Lords held that duress from another person is no defence to murder, including for those who assist. See pp. 429 and 439, and Ward LJ’s discussion of Howe in part IV.7.3 of Re A .
  • Re A (Children) [2000] EWCA Civ 254. The Court of Appeal allowed surgery to separate conjoined twins. The three judges emphasised, respectively, reasoning similar to defence of another, the requirements of necessity, and the purpose of the operation together with its foreseeable and unavoidable consequence. See part IV.7 of Ward LJ’s opinion, the section on necessity in Brooke LJ’s opinion, and the end of Robert Walker LJ’s opinion. Full judgment · Contemporary reporting on the hospital’s announcement
  • Parker’s memorial. The memorial in the churchyard at Pear Tree Church in Southampton still bears Parker’s name, age, and date of death. Church history · Transcription of the inscription
